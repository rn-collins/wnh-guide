The Six Assessment Dimensions

Every NSAG assessment has six dimensions — specific, observable, documentable governance criteria. For WNH, those six dimensions are:

D1

Sex-Disaggregated Evidence Standards

Does the guidance, protocol, or clinical material distinguish between male and female neurobiological responses — receptor density, metabolic pathways, adverse event profiles — rather than applying general population data to female patients?

Why it matters: The female nervous system is not a male nervous system with a different hormone profile. Applying male-dominant research to female patients is not a minor limitation — it is a systematic clinical governance failure with documented adverse event consequences.

D2

Life-Stage Differentiation

Does the framework account for the distinct neurobiological contexts of female life stages — adolescence, reproductive years (including cycle phase variability), perimenopause, and post-menopause — as governance-relevant variables affecting dosing, risk, and therapeutic response?

Why it matters: Endocannabinoid tone, psychiatric risk profiles, and psychedelic response are not static across a woman's life. A governance framework that treats a 22-year-old and a 52-year-old identically on these questions is not applying the available evidence.

D3

Disclosure Safety Infrastructure

What is the safety of the environment for a patient or client to disclose cannabis or psychedelic use? Are there documented policies on confidentiality, regulatory reporting, and clinical response that make disclosure a safe act?

Why it matters: The documentation gap between cannabis use prevalence and EHR documentation (35% vs 5%) exists because disclosure is not safe in most clinical environments. Disclosure safety is a design variable that the institution controls.

D4

Clinician Training and Competency Standards

Are clinicians trained on sex-disaggregated neuroscience for cannabis and psychedelics? Can they answer female patient questions with primary-source accuracy? Do they know what they don't know?

Why it matters: Most clinicians are asked about cannabis and psychedelics by patients who are already using these substances. Most have no primary-source training on female-specific neurological effects. The clinician knowledge gap is a governance problem with patient safety consequences.

D5

Psychiatric Risk Communication

Are sex-differentiated psychiatric risk profiles — for psychosis, anxiety, dependency acceleration, and perimenopause-specific interactions — communicated to patients at the appropriate specificity and with appropriate evidence grading?

Why it matters: Sex differences in psychiatric adverse events from cannabis and psychedelics are documented in primary research. Communicating only general population risk data to female patients is not full informed consent. It is informed consent with a systematic evidence gap.

D6

Patient-Facing Communication Standards

Are patient-facing materials, guides, and educational resources sex-disaggregated rather than gender-neutral? Do they reflect the female-specific neuroscience, or do they apply general population research with a female-presenting wrapper?

Why it matters: Gender-neutral materials systematically obscure sex-specific information women need to make informed decisions about substances that interact with their nervous systems differently. Communication design is a governance variable.

Annotated Evidence Library

Primary Sources, Actually Read

Annotated primary sources — what they found, why it matters, and what the evidence grade is. Not summaries of summaries.

Cannabis - Receptor Biology - 2013

Estrous cycle influences cannabinoid receptor mRNA expression — Craft et al.

Demonstrated that CB1 receptor density and mRNA expression vary across the estrous cycle in female rodents, with progesterone-related fluctuations affecting cannabinoid sensitivity. Foundational for understanding why female cannabis response is not simply a scaled version of male response. Evidence grade: B (animal model; human replication studies ongoing).

Drug and Alcohol Dependence, 2013 | DOI: 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2012.11.009

Cannabis - Sex Differences - Clinical - 2016

Sex differences in self-reported marijuana effects and self-administration — Cooper and Haney

Human clinical study showing females develop cannabis dependence more rapidly than males (telescoping effect), report greater subjective drug effects at lower doses, and show higher rates of anxiety as an adverse event. One of the few human primary sources on sex-differentiated cannabis response. Evidence grade: B (controlled lab setting; sample size limitations).

Neuropsychopharmacology, 2016 | DOI: 10.1038/npp.2015.302

Psychedelics - Sex Differences - 2021

Sex differences in psilocybin pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties — Shao et al.

Early-stage human data suggesting sex differences in psilocybin metabolism and subjective effects. Important primarily as a signal that landmark psilocybin trials aggregate results cannot be applied to female patients without considering sex as a moderating variable. Evidence grade: Moderate-Test (small sample, early data).

Frontiers in Pharmacology, 2021 | DOI: 10.3389/fphar.2021.625348

Perimenopause - Cannabis - 2022

Cannabis use in perimenopausal and menopausal women: patterns, motives, and perceived effects

Survey-based primary source documenting that perimenopausal women report cannabis use primarily for sleep, anxiety, and pain — and that their perceived efficacy diverges from general population findings in ways suggesting hormonal context moderates response. Evidence grade: C (self-report; no objective outcome measures). Cited because the gap between this and the clinical evidence base is itself clinically important.

Menopause, 2022 | DOI: 10.1097/GME.0000000000001972

Signal vs. Noise

What Is Worth Paying Attention To

Signal

Sex-disaggregated cannabis research: CB1 receptor density and estrogen interactions

A growing body of primary research documents that CB1 receptor density varies across the menstrual cycle and differs from male baseline patterns — affecting both therapeutic response and adverse event profiles. This research is not incorporated into most clinical guidance. Key primary sources: Craft et al. (2013, Drug and Alcohol Dependence), Cooper and Haney (2016, Neuropsychopharmacology).

Signal

FDA sex-as-a-biological-variable (SABV) policy implementation studies

The FDA 2016 mandate to include sex as a biological variable in preclinical research is producing new primary data on sex-specific drug interactions. Monitoring this literature provides earlier signal on female-specific findings than waiting for clinical guidelines to update. Primary source: FDA Docket FDA-2014-D-0508.

Noise

Womens wellness content from supplement and cannabis brands

This content systematically overstates the evidence for product categories the brand sells. The research cited is often real but cherry-picked and presented at a confidence level the underlying studies do not support. It is marketing dressed as clinical guidance.

Noise

General psychedelic clinical trial results applied without sex disaggregation

Most landmark psychedelic trials (MAPS MDMA studies, Johns Hopkins psilocybin work) enrolled female participants but did not publish sex-disaggregated outcomes as primary findings. Applying aggregate results to female patients without acknowledging this limitation is a clinical judgment error, not just a data gap.

What Applying the Framework Looks Like

Hypothetical examples drawn from real decision types in WNH.

Before

Dosing guidance: Start low, go slow

A clinician recommends cannabis dosing to a female patient in her late 30s using general population guidance: start with 2.5mg THC, titrate slowly. The guidance is not wrong, but it does not account for documented interactions between THC sensitivity and luteal phase progesterone levels — meaning the same dose will have measurably different effects depending on where the patient is in her cycle.

After NSAG Framework

Cycle-aware dosing protocol with documented rationale

The same recommendation includes a documented note: THC sensitivity is elevated during the luteal phase (days 15-28) due to progesterone-CB1 interactions documented in Craft et al. (2013). The clinician flags this to the patient and suggests tracking response by cycle phase for the first 60 days. The guidance is evidence-based, female-specific, and transferable.

Before

Educational content: Psychedelics may help with depression

A platform publishes content about psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression citing the Johns Hopkins and MAPS trials. The content does not note that these trials enrolled participants across sexes and did not publish sex-disaggregated outcomes, leaving readers unable to assess how applicable the findings are to female-specific neurobiological context.

After NSAG Framework

Sex-disaggregated framing with evidence grade

The same content includes a WNH evidence note: These trials enrolled female participants; sex-disaggregated outcomes were not a primary reported finding. The aggregate results are promising. Female-specific replication studies are underway. Until published, applying these findings to female patients requires clinical judgment about individual hormonal and life-stage context. Confidence grade: Moderate-Test.

Terminology

WNH Glossary

Language used precisely in this domain. These distinctions carry real analytical weight.

Sex-disaggregated evidence

Research findings reported separately by biological sex, enabling clinicians and researchers to assess whether effects, dosing, and risk profiles differ between male and female populations. Distinct from gender-sensitive research, which addresses social factors.

Endocannabinoid tone

The baseline level of endocannabinoid system activity in an individual. In female physiology, endocannabinoid tone fluctuates across the menstrual cycle and across life stages, affecting both baseline mood regulation and response to exogenous cannabinoids.

Luteal phase

Days 15-28 of the menstrual cycle, following ovulation. Progesterone dominates this phase. Progesterone metabolites are positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A receptors, which affects CNS sensitivity to cannabinoids and other substances during this window — a clinically relevant variable that most substance use guidance ignores.

Psychiatric risk differential

The variation in risk for specific psychiatric outcomes (psychosis, anxiety, depersonalization) between population subgroups. For cannabis, females show higher rates of certain adverse psychiatric events at equivalent doses compared to male controls in controlled studies.

Primary source vs. clinical guideline

A primary source is original research (a published study, trial, or dataset). A clinical guideline is a synthesized recommendation derived from multiple primary sources. Guidelines lag primary sources by years. WNH works from primary sources because the female-specific literature is newer than most existing guidelines.
